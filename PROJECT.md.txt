EgoLabel: Project Spec for the Meta Global AI Developer Hackathon
Prepared 27 October 2026 · Indie developer track · Build window closes 4 November 2026
1. One-line pitch
EgoLabel turns raw first-person (egocentric) video into robot-ready labels: task steps, hand and object masks, and hand-object contact. It does this automatically, then asks a human to review only the parts the AI is unsure about.
Hackathon fit
• Theme: Push the Frontier (physical AI and robotics data). Confirm the exact theme names on the submission form.
• Track: Indie developer.
• Meta products used together: SAM 3.1 (hand and object masks, tracking through video) and Muse Wonder (watches the video and writes the step timeline). Muse Spark is an optional cheap helper for cleanup.
2. The problem in plain words
Robotics companies train robots from first-person video of people doing tasks. Humans currently label that video by hand: when each step starts and ends, where the hands and objects are, and whether a hand is touching an object. One vendor estimates hand-object labeling costs 15 to 30 times the length of the video. Hand-object contact is the most important label and the one most often done wrong. EgoLabel cuts the manual work and shows how accurate the AI actually is.
3. What the judges must see (90-second demo)
1. Upload a 30 to 60 second first-person clip (for example, making tea).
2. Within about a minute the screen shows the video with colored hand and object outlines, and a timeline of steps underneath.
3. Some steps are flagged yellow: AI unsure, please review.
4. The reviewer fixes one flagged step in two clicks.
5. Click Export: a clean labels file downloads.
6. Final slide: measured accuracy against human labels, and time saved (see section 7).
Why this earns points: it is visual (outlines moving on video), it works end to end, it uses two Meta models together, and it reports real accuracy numbers instead of only claiming it works.
4. Scope
Must have (the MVP)
• Upload one video, process it, show the results.
• Step timeline from Muse Wonder.
• Hand masks and object masks from SAM 3.1, shown over the video.
• Contact on/off estimate for each step.
• Flag uncertain items for review. The reviewer can edit label text and step times.
• Export labels as JSON.
• Accuracy report on 3 to 5 clips you labeled by hand.
Should have (only if the MVP is stable by 1 November)
• Second processing run for disagreement checks (see section 5).
• CSV export.
• Side-by-side view: AI labels vs human labels.
Will not do
• No 3D, depth, eye-gaze or sensor data.
• No user accounts, payments, databases or cloud hosting. Run it on your own laptop.
• No training of any robot model.
• No marketplace or agency features. That is a later business, not this build.
5. How it works
1. Upload. The user picks a clip. The app saves it locally.
2. Frame extraction. A free tool called ffmpeg pulls out frames at 2 per second, each with its timestamp.
3. Step timeline (Muse Wonder). Send the video, or the timestamped frames if video upload is not supported, with a prompt asking for task steps as structured data: start time, end time, short label, objects involved.
4. Masks (SAM 3.1). Ask SAM 3.1 to outline hand and each object name that Muse Wonder mentioned, then track them through the frames. If text prompts are not supported, use point or box prompts instead. Check the docs.
5. Contact estimate. Rule: if a hand outline touches or overlaps an object outline for 3 or more sampled frames in a row, call it contact. For unclear cases, ask Muse Wonder about that key frame: is the hand touching the object?
6. Flagging. Mark a step needs_review if any one of these is true: (a) two separate Muse Wonder runs, using different frame sampling, disagree on that step; (b) SAM loses the object or the outline size jumps suddenly; (c) contact is borderline. Do not rely on the model's own confidence numbers. Agreement between runs is more trustworthy.
7. Review screen. Video player, timeline, outline overlay, flagged items in yellow, edit and accept buttons.
8. Export. One JSON file per clip.
6. Output format (what the exported file contains)
• clip_id and duration
• steps: id, start time, end time, label, objects, needs_review (yes or no), reviewer_edited (yes or no)
• contact_events: start time, end time, which hand, which object, needs_review
• review summary: number of edits, seconds spent reviewing
7. Evaluation: what turns this from a demo into evidence
Record 5 clips. Label 3 to 5 of them by hand first. This is your ground truth. Then measure:
• Step boundary error: average seconds between the AI step times and your step times.
• Step label match: percent of steps where the AI label means the same as yours.
• Contact accuracy: percent of 1-second checkpoints where AI contact on/off matches yours.
• Time saved: stopwatch one clip labeled from scratch vs the same clip reviewed after the AI. Write both numbers down.
Report the numbers honestly, even if they are mediocre. Judges trust a tool that shows its weak spots more than one that claims to be perfect. Also list 2 or 3 failure examples and what you would fix next.
8. Technology choices (kept simple on purpose)
• Backend: Python with FastAPI.
• Video tool: ffmpeg.
• Screen: one HTML page with plain JavaScript (video player plus a canvas layer for outlines). If this takes more than a day, fall back to Streamlit.
• Storage: plain files on disk. Save every API result to disk so the demo never depends on live API calls.
• Meta access: Meta Model API. Reported model IDs are muse-wonder-1.0 and muse-spark-1.3, and the reported base URL is https://api.meta.ai/v1. Treat these as unverified and check the official docs first. Each participant reportedly gets 150 dollars of credits, and costs for short clips should be small.
9. Data plan
• Record 5 to 8 clips of 30 to 60 seconds with a phone strapped to your chest or head. One simple tabletop task each: making tea, packing a small box, folding a towel, assembling a simple object, cooking something easy.
• Use good light, keep hands clearly visible, and avoid other people's faces.
• Use clips you recorded yourself. Public datasets such as Ego4D and EPIC-Kitchens come with licenses, so skip them unless you read the terms.
• Hand-label 3 to 5 of your clips in a spreadsheet: step start, step end, label, and contact on/off at 1-second marks.

2. Facts from Meta's docs, and what is still unverified
From Meta's docs pages (dev.meta.ai/docs/overview, /docs/models, /docs/pricing-rate-limits), found 28 Oct:
• Base URL https://api.meta.ai/v1 with a Bearer key.
• muse-wonder-1.0 takes text, image, video, audio and PDF and returns text. It has a 1,048,576-token context window, always reasons, and has reasoning effort levels from minimal to max. Price: 4 dollars per million input tokens, 0.20 cached input, 20 output. Standard mode only.
• Muse Spark (for example muse-spark-1.2) also takes video input and is cheaper: 1.25 input and 4.25 output per million tokens on the Standard tier. The contributor variants are cheaper still, but Meta may train on your prompts, so do not use them.
• sam-3.1: POST /v1/responses; upload clips with POST /v1/files; a short noun phrase prompt per request; returns boxes and masks and follows objects across video frames. Price: 0.20 dollars per 1,000 video frames, and each video request is billed at least 50 frames.
• Rate limits: 3,000 requests per minute per team per model.
Unverified, test on Day 1:
• Exact request and response shapes. The agent must read the docs and not guess.
• Wonder tokens per second of video, latency, and whether reasoning tokens inflate the bill (check the usage field).
• Files API size limit, and whether SAM bills frames at the native frame rate (we downsample to 6 fps before upload to be safe).
• Vercel Hobby limits: reported 300 seconds function time and 4.5 MB request body, personal and non-commercial use only. Check Vercel's current docs.
• That your 150 dollar credit covers Wonder, Spark and SAM. Check the dashboard.
3. Which models we use, and why no fine-tuning
• Perception and labeling: Meta models only, called through the API. Muse Wonder writes the step timeline and answers contact questions on hand crops. SAM 3.1 finds and tracks hands and objects. Muse Spark is a second, cheaper opinion on the step timeline.
• Our own small logic: voting, rules, thresholds, and one small classifier (logistic regression or similar) that decides hand-object contact from numbers the Meta models produce. This is our code, not a Meta model. The report must say so.
• No fine-tuning. I found nothing showing that the Meta Model API offers fine-tuning for Wonder or SAM, so do not plan on it. SAM 3.1 weights are open, but fine-tuning them needs a GPU, a labeled training set far larger than 10 clips, and days of work we do not have. Accuracy comes from the levers in section 7 instead: closed vocabulary, examples in the prompt, hand crops, two-model agreement, the small contact classifier, and routing by trust score.
4. Architecture
• Browser: the user picks a clip. The browser re-encodes it to about 540p, 6 fps, no audio, target 4 MB or less. That fits Vercel's request limit and keeps SAM billing low.
• Vercel API routes (Next.js, TypeScript): upload to the Meta Files API; call Wonder and Spark for steps; call SAM once per phrase (hand, plus each object from the step list) in parallel; return results. The key stays on the server.
• Quality engine (TypeScript): agreement checks, contact rule, scorer weights loaded from a JSON file, flagging and routing.
• Lab (Python, on your laptop): runs the benchmark on the 10 clips, caches every API response, computes metrics, trains the contact classifier, and writes config JSON that the app loads.
• Storage: no database. Live sessions use browser storage. Sample clips and their real results are static files in the repo.
• If compression hurts accuracy: upload with Vercel Blob (check its free allowance) and let the server stream the file to Meta.

6. Data and labeling
Closed vocabulary (starter, adjust to what you can film):
• Make tea: pick up kettle, pour water into mug, place kettle down, open tea box, place tea bag in mug, stir with spoon. Objects: kettle, mug, tea box, tea bag, spoon.
• Pack a box: open box flaps, pick up item, place item in box, close flaps, apply tape. Objects: box, item, tape.
• Fold a towel: pick up towel, spread towel flat, fold in half, fold in thirds, place on stack. Object: towel.
Filming: phone on chest or head, landscape, 1080p, good light, hands in view, 30 to 45 seconds, no other people. Record 10 clips, about 3 per task, mixing speed and lighting. If you can get access, also record 2 or 3 clips in a real workshop, shop or kitchen with messy light, label them too, and report them separately as a harder set. Clips 1 to 5 are the tuning set and clips 6 to 10 are the test set. Never tune on test clips.
Ground truth sheet 1 columns: clip_id, step_label, start_s, end_s. Sheet 2 columns: clip_id, second, hand_object_contact (yes or no), object. Label contact at every 1-second mark. Budget about 2 hours for 10 clips.
7. How accuracy is measured and improved
Measures: median step boundary error in seconds; step label match percent; contact balanced accuracy and F1 at 1-second marks, next to a baseline that always guesses the most common answer; coverage (percent auto-accepted); selective accuracy (accuracy on auto-accepted items).
Every metric is also reported for the raw-model baseline: one plain prompt to Muse Wonder, with no vocabulary, no second model and no crops. The gap between the raw model and our pipeline is the evidence that our work adds value. If the gap is small, say so.
The 95 percent claim means accuracy on auto-accepted items at or above 95 percent on the test clips, with coverage reported next to it. Fully automatic accuracy is reported separately. If any figure falls short, report the real figure.
Levers, in order of expected gain: closed vocabulary; two-model agreement (Wonder and Spark) instead of one; hand crops for contact questions; examples of each step in the prompt; a small trained contact classifier; threshold chosen on tuning clips; error analysis loop where reviewer corrections become prompt examples.
